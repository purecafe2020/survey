Why One Study Pays $20 and Another Pays $300

People assume pay tracks difficulty or length. It does not. It tracks how expensive you are to replace.

Verified：November 2026 About 2 min read

Variable one: how scarce you are

A study recruiting "adults who drink coffee" has an effectively unlimited pool and pays accordingly. A study recruiting "procurement leads at mid-sized manufacturers who renewed an ERP contract in the last year" has a pool of perhaps forty people nationally, and pays ten to twenty times more for the same hour.

Scarcity is the largest single factor, and it is the only one you can influence — by accurately describing what makes you unusual.

Variable two: what the answer is worth

If your answer informs a $50 packaging decision, the budget is small. If it informs a $2 million software purchase, the client will happily pay $300 for an hour and consider it cheap.

This is why B2B and healthcare studies sit at the top of every rate table: the decisions behind them are expensive.

Variable three: the method

MethodWhy it pays what it does
SurveyReplaces you easily; pays cents per minute
Unmoderated testOne recording, no scheduling; $10-60
InterviewLive follow-ups, scheduled slot; $40-150
Focus groupSynchronised six to eight people; $100-350
Diary studyMulti-day compliance; $89-300 total

Variable four: who is buying

The same study costs more when a client recruits through a platform with published terms than when a panel aggregates cheap traffic. Intermediaries take a cut, and the rate you see is what survives it.

This is also why "up to $50 a survey" advertising is technically true and practically useless: it quotes the best case from the rarest study type.

What to do with this

  1. Chase scarcity, not volume. Ten well-described unusual attributes beat a thousand five-minute surveys.
  2. Prefer session-priced work. If the fee is stated per session, the pricing is transparent and the ceiling is high.
  3. Ignore per-minute arithmetic on panels. A three-dollar survey is three dollars no matter how the site frames the "hourly equivalent".
  4. Re-price your own time. Once you know an interview pays $100 for an hour, a $4 survey becomes visibly not worth opening.

Frequently asked

Why is one study $5 and another $200?

Audience scarcity and decision value. The $200 study needs a rare person and feeds an expensive decision; the $5 one needs anyone.

Are high-paying studies real?

Yes. Interviews and focus groups regularly pay $40-350 through platforms with published terms. They are just harder to qualify for.

How do I get the better-paying ones?

Describe your work accurately and specifically, keep the profile current, and apply early — then accept that most applications will not convert.
